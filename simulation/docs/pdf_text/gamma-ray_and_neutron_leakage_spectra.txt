GAMMA-RAY AND NEUTRON LEAKAGE SPECTRA CALCULATED FOR UNSHIELDED REACTORS 
by 
JAMES TERRELL 
ABSTRACT 
The spectra of neutrons and gamma rays escaping 
from unshielded reactors have been calculated for a 
n'omber of simplified cases. Such spectra are important 
in connection with reactors operating in space orbit 
around the earth, which would normally have little or 
no heavy shielding. Reactors in space, such as the 
Soviet RORSAT spacecraft, have caused considerable 
interference with the operations of other spacecraft. 
Knowledge of the characteristics of their leakage 
spectra may be useful in understanding or minimizing 
such interference. 
The Monte 
Carlo Neutron-Photon 
(MCNP) code 
developed at Los Alamos has been used in these 
calculations. 
In the cases considered here the 
critical assembly is assumed to have spherically 
symmetrical 
geometry, 
with 
a 
central 
core 
of 
fissionable material surrounded by one or more shells 
of other material. The outer shells considered include 
beryllium, beryllium oxide, sodium, potassium, lithium, 
lithium hydride, and iron. 
The results obtained, 
presented as graphs, show that a number of materials 
that may be used in space reactors should lead to 
distinctive gamma-ray and neutron leakage spectra. 
Measurements of such spectra might well be useful in 
characterizing an unknown reactor type. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The leakage spectra from unshielded nuclear reactors are important 
in connection with reactors operating in orbital space. A number of 
such reactors have already been put into use, primarily in Soviet Radar 
Ocean Reconnaissance Satellites (RORSATs). Reactor-powered satellites, 
such as the SP-100 test reactor, may be launched again by the United 
States. Such reactors would normally have little no shielding because 
of weight considerations. 
Little experimental work has been done on leakage spectra, it 
appears. Unshielded reactors are difficult to work with; such critical 
assemblies as Godiva, used at Los Alamos for many years, emit neutrons 
and gamma rays in a very short burst, leading to major problems in 
measuring the spectra. The calculations of such spectra that have been 

===== PAGE 7 =====
done have been primarily of the group variety, in which the energy 
groups typically cover many MeV, and are not useful in precise 
evaluation of spectra from different types of reactors. A significant 
feature of an unshielded reactor is that the spectra are characteristic 
of the reactor type rather than of the shielding, and are furthermore 
of high intensity and relatively undegraded energy. 
Unplanned observations of gamma-radiation from orbiting Soviet 
Cosmos/RORSAT reactors, or of positrons produced by the gamma rays, 
have been made, however, starting in 1980. The declassified results of 
such observations, made by means of the gamma-ray spectrometer on the 
Solar Maximum Mission satellite, have recently been published (Rieger 
et al. 1989, Share et al. 1989, Hones and Higbie 1989). In addition, a 
balloon-borne gamma-ray telescope has made spectral measurements of 
several simultaneously-orbiting Soviet reactors (O'Neill et al. 1989). 
It is not clear that individual gamma-ray lines can be identified in 
these spectra, except for the 511-keV positron annihilation line. 
However, the number and intensity of these orbiting sources have been 
sufficient to interfere considerably with the operations of Solar 
Maximum Mission, Ginga, and perhaps other satellites. 
The purpose of the present calculations is to give some idea of 
the neutron and gamma-ray spectra to be expected from various types of 
unshielded reactors. 
The Monte Carlo Neutron-Photon (MCNP) program 
developed at Los Alamos has been used in these calculations. 
The 
results obtained show that a number of materials that may be used in 
reactors should lead to quite distinctive spectra. 
II. THE MCNP PROGRAM 
The MCNP program—a Monte Carlo code for neutron and photon 
transport—was developed at Los Alamos over the course of many years. 
In recent years its development has been due to the efforts of the 
Radiation Transport Group (X-6) of the Los Alamos National Laboratory 
(Briesmeister 1986). 
MCNP follows the paths of large numbers of 
individual neutrons and photons through a specified three-dimensional 
material assembly by means of random choices of type of interaction 
(fission, scattering, absorption,...), path length, scattering angle, 
and so on. 
The cross sections that determine the probabilities are 
based on ENDF/B-V (Evaluated Neutron Data File) evaluations (Garber 
1979), and are intended to represent the best knowledge—or guesses—as 
to the neutron and photon interactions involved (Soran and Seamon 
1980). 
For photons the types of interaction used include incoherent and 
coherent scattering, pair production with emission of annihilation 
radiation, 
and photoelectric 
absorption 
followed 
by 
fluorescent 
emission of lower-energy photons. The random choices made in the Monte 
Carlo calculation result in spectra that have the same sort of counting 
fluctuations in each energy channel as in actual or hypothetical 
measurements, without, it is hoped, the systematic 
errors that 
characterize actual detectors. Standard deviations are calculated for 
each energy channel, based on the weighted number of counts. 
The

results in this paper are presented primarily as graphs, with a 
resolution of 10 keV in most cases, except where noted. 
It should be noted that these MCNP calculations take no account of 
delayed fission gamma rays, fission-product gamma rays, or gamma rays 
from fissile materials (uranium and plutonium) or from reactor 
materials activated by neutrons. The effect of delayed fission and 
fission product gamma rays, in particular, would be to raise the level 
of the continuum. It is hoped that these omitted photons would not 
greatly alter the leakage spectra of a reactor in space, but in any 
case they would depend on the power and operational history of the 
reactor.

have spherically symmetric geometry, with a central core of fissionable 
material surrounded by one or more shells of other material. The outer

criticality to the assembly. The results obtained show that a number 
of materials that may be used in reactors should lead to quite 
distinctive neutron or photon spectra. 
III. COMPUTED REACTOR LEAKAGE SPECTRA: ENRICHED URANIUM (GODIVA) 
The simplest spherically-symmetrical case is a bare sphere of 
fissionable material, such as the Godiva critical assembly that has 
been extensively investigated at Los Alamos. 
This assembly, in 
idealized form, is a sphere of enriched uranium, taken here to be 
93.77% 235u, 5.20% ^ssy^ ^nd 1.03% 234u (atom fractions), with a radius 
of 8.7407 cm. 
With the density taken to be 18.742 g/cm^, this 
corresponds to a mass of 52.43 kg. These specifications are based on 
the experimental determination of the critical mass for Godiva, after 
correction for voids, reflection, and other departures from ideal 
assumptions; the result was a critical mass of 52.43 ± 0.23 kg for the 
density assumed here (Hansen and Paxton 1969). 
The multiplication 
factor (neutron multiplication per generation) for this geometry, for 
40 generations of 3000 neutrons each, was calculated by the MCNP code 
to be k = 0.997 ± 0.002 (the uncertainties used in this report are 
standard deviations). 
Determination of the critical mass was not a high priority of the 
present calculations, but it ' is encouraging to note the excellent 
agreement of calculation and experiment: the value of k should be 1.0 
for a critical mass. The neutron and gamma-ray leakage spectra, which 
are of most interest in this report, should be independent of the 
density for a critical mass of given material in a specified shape. 
Thus the dimensions, densities, and critical masses assumed in the 
calculations presented here should have no effect on the leakage 
spectra of photons and neutrons, so long as the the assembly is 
critical and the relative radii and densities are unchanged.

In MCNP 
calculations the fission gamma-ray spectrum is smooth and approximately 
exponential (Stewart and Hunter 1972), but only for fission induced by 
neutrons of less than 1.08 MeV energy. For neutrons of higher energy 
the photon spectrum produced has steps at 0.5-MeV energy intervals, 
above 1 MeV. Some evidence may be seen in Fig. 2 of these steps, 
artifacts of the calculation. 
They smooth out and vanish with 
sufficient scattering material outside the fission source. 
The total yield of gamma rays is 0.137 ± 0.002 photons per fission 
neutron, or 0.358 photons per fission, using v = 2.616 neutrons/fission 
for this case. This value of v is given by the MCNP calcuation for 
this fast reactor, and is of course higher than the thermal fission 
value of 2.43; the value of v increases by about 1 neutron per 7 MeV of 
energy of the neutrons inducing fission (Terrell 1962, 1965). 
Figure 3 shows the neutron leakage spectrum calculated for the 
Godiva fast reactor assembly. It is a slightly degraded version of the 
fission neutron spectrum, with no other characteristic features 
apparent. 
The fission neutron spectrum is well represented by a 
Maxwellian spectrum, N(E) = E^/^e 
' , in which E is the neutron energy 
and T is a parameter equal to 2/3 of the average energy (Terrell 1959, 
1962). The average energy of fission neutrons is about 2 MeV, and the 
maximum in the spectrum is at about 1/3 of the average energy, as 
expected for a Maxwellian distribution of fission neutrons. For Godiva 
the peak in the neutron leakage spectrum occurs at a lower energy, 
-0.45 MeV from this calculation. However, the spectrum retains its 
exponential character, as seen in Fig. 4, a semi-logarithmic plot of 
the data of Fig. 3, with 100-keV resolution.

V. 2 35u CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
The results to be presented in the balance of this report are 
based on a spherical critical assembly of pure ^ssy (or uranium nitride 
in one case), surrounded by concentric spherical shells of various 
materials. The choice of pure 235u ^j^s made, not because this material 
is apt to be used, but for computational simplicity. 
No obvious 
difference in the resulting spectra has been found, as may be seen in 
Figs. 7 and 8. The spherical radius is taken to be 8.5 cm, slightly 
smaller than that assumed for the Godiva assembly. This is found to 
give an appropriate critical multiplication factor, k = 0.999 ± 0.003, 
for a density of 18.73 g/cm^ and a total mass of 48.18 kg. 
The photon spectrum is shown in Fig. 7, with the 10-keV resolution 
used in most of these calculations. In this case as in others, the 
spectrum above 4 MeV is not shown if there are no significant features 
above this energy. 
Figure 8 shows the neutron leakage spectrum from the 2 35u 
assembly. These results for the neutron spectrum, as for the photon 
spectrum, are very similar to those for Godiva. 
VI. 235UN CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
The leakage spectra from a sphere of uranium nitride were 
investigated as a slightly more complicated case. This material, in 
pellet form, may be used in the SP-100 space nuclear reactor. After 
several preliminary trials, a 12.0-cm sphere of ^BSy^ was specified, at 
a density of 13.6 g/cm^; the nitrogen was assumed to be ^^N (the 
natural isotopic mixture is 99.63% ^^N). An MCNP run of 20 generations 
of 3000 neutrons each then gave the multiplication factor as 
k = 1.029 ± 0.003. Thus the assumed mass of 98.44 kg (including 92.90 
kg of uranium) turned out to be somewhat above critical mass; probably 
11.7 cm would have been the best choice of radius.

VII. 235u -I- Li CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
The effect of surrounding a uranium sphere with a spherical shell 
of lithium was investigated, using the same methods as in the simpler 
cases. 
Lithium metal has been, used or proposed as a coolant for 
various reactors, including SP-100. For the present calculation, a 
shell of natural lithium with a thickness of 10 cm and density 
p = 0.534 g/cm^ is assumed to surround the 235u sphere, with no gap. 
The uranium, with a density taken to be p = 18.73 g/cm^, was varied in 
radius until an approximately critical size was determined. 
The 
resulting radius of 8.0 cm corresponds to a critical mass of 40.17 kg. 
An MCNP calculation involving 40 generations of 3000 neutrons each gave 
the multiplication factor as k = 1.005 ± 0.002. 
The spectrum of escaping gamma rays, shown in Fig. 11, is similar 
to that found for bare spheres of 2 35u or ^^^?u, 
except for one 
conspicuous difference—the huge gamma-ray peak at 0.478 MeV. 
The spectrum of neutrons leaking from this hypothetical reactor is 
shown in Fig. 12, and is obviously much more distinctive (due to 
scattering in lithium) than the bare-assembly cases discussed above. 
It has a large low-energy peak at about 170 keV, followed by a 
conspicuous dip. 
VIII. 235u -I- 7LiH CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
The effect of LiH as a moderator (or shadow shield, as proposed 
for SP-100) on these leakage spectra was investigated in another set of 
runs. The lithium was assumed to be isotopically separated ''Li, in 
order to avoid the propensity of ^Li for capturing neutrons. The 
lithium hydride shell was taken to be 10 cm thick at a density of 
p =0.74 g/cm^, and the radius of the uranium core (with density taken 
to be 18.73 g/cm^) was adjusted until the assembly was approximately 
critical. This resulted in a core radius of 6.7 cm and a corresponding 
mass of 23.60 kg, yielding k = 1.003 ± 0.002 for a 40-generation run of 
3000 neutrons.

XI. 235u + Be CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
The effect of berylliimi as a reflector of leakage neutrons was 
investigated in another MCNP run, involving 29 generations of 6000 
neutrons each; this run, involving more than the usual number of 
neutron tracks, took 19.7 minutes of Cray-1 time. It was assumed that 
the 2 35u core (p = 18.73 g/cm^) was surrounded by a spherical shell of 
^Be, 10 cm thick, at a density of 1.848 g/cm^. 
Criticality of this 
assembly (k = 0.997 ± 0.002) was found to occur for a core radius of 
5.6 cm of uranium (13.78 kg). Thus the beryllium reflector had a large 
effect in decreasing the critical mass of uranium, as expected, from 
the unreflected value of 48.18 kg. 
The resultant gammia-ray spectrum, shown in Figs. 19 and 20, is 
considerably different from previous cases. 
This and other cases 
involving beryllium are characterized by strong downscattering of the 
escaping photons. 
The spectrum of escaping neutrons, shown in Fig. 21, is also quite 
different from previous cases, with the exception of ''LiH (Fig. 14) . 
Strong scattering of fission neutrons produces a very high peak at low 
energies, off scale in this figure. Thus the neutron spectrum for a 
beryllium-reflected critical assembly should be quite distinctive, and 
would doubtless be so even for a thinner reflector.

XV. 235u-i-Fe + Be CRITICAL ASSEMBLY 
A final MCNP run was made for a reactor assembly including iron, 
since this element is likely to be used as structural material. The 
iron was taken to be present as a 5-cm thick shell (p = 7.874 g/cm^), 
surrounded by an outer shell of beryllium (p = 1.848 g/cm^) also 5 cm

Criticality was found for a 235u core of 6.5 cm radius 
(k = 1.002 ± 0.002), giving a critical mass of 21.55 kg. The MCNP run 
involved 50 generations of 3000 neutrons. 
The escaping photon spectrum, seen in Figs. 31 and 32, is 
conspicuous for the large number of sharp spectral lines, mostly due to 
inelastic neutron scattering in iron. The most conspicuous feature of 
the spectrum is the strong inelastic scattering line at 847 keV, but 
numerous other inelastic lines are also visible. 
Four beryllium 
capture lines are noticeable. It is not clear why some beryllium lines

differences in the spectrum of neutrons interacting, particularly if 
nonthermal 
reactions 
occur, or 
statistical 
fluctuations may be 
responsible in some cases. 
The broad peak around 100 keV is due to Compton downscattering in 
the beryllium, as has been verified by determination of the spectrum of 
photons entering the iron from the outer beryllium shell (not shown). 
The photon spectrum leaving the uranium has prominent fluorescence

The spectrum of neutrons escaping from this assembly is shown in

using beryllium reflectors.

XVI. CONCLUSIONS 
The numerous hypothetical 
reactor 
(critical assembly) cases 
considered in this paper are not intended to be exhaustive, merely 
representative. 
It is not likely that any of these graphs will 
coincide with actual experimental data when obtained, particularly in 
terms of available energy resolution, but it is hoped that some 
features at least will turn out to be characteristic of the reactor 
materials. 
The distinctive features shown here in many neutron or 
photon spectra may well be useful in characterizing an unknown reactor 
type if data should become available.
